How Sunoto works

Tap Right Option, talk, and tap it again. Your words appear wherever your cursor is: an email, a Slack message, a doc, a terminal. This page explains what happens in between, in plain language.

Stays on your computer

The speech engine and the AI editor run on the laptop itself. No internet connection, account, or subscription is involved.

Fast

Text arrives about a quarter second after your second tap, or about half a second with the AI editor switched on.

Works in any app

Sunoto pastes the text the way you would, so it works anywhere you can type. Apps need no plugin or integration.

The parts

Sunoto is one main program, the coordinator, plus three helper programs that it starts and talks to. The coordinator does the quick, simple jobs itself: it listens for the shortcut, records the microphone, applies the tidy-up rules, and pastes the result. The heavy or screen-related jobs go to helpers.

Why separate helper programs? Two reasons that matter for the product. First, if a helper crashes, the coordinator restarts it and dictation keeps working; a crash never takes Sunoto down. Second, the team can swap a helper without touching the rest. The Mac and Linux versions use different speech engines behind the same coordinator.

One sentence, start to finish

Here is a real example, run through Sunoto’s cleanup on 15 October 2026. Someone corrects themselves mid-sentence, which is exactly what people do when they talk.

  1. 1
    Tap Right Option and talk · coordinator

    The status pill appears with a red dot, a sound meter, and “Tap again to paste, Esc to cancel”. The speech engine starts working while you are still talking, which is why the result arrives so quickly after the second tap. Esc throws the recording away.

    You say “Um, call him at five, actually, at five thirty.”
  2. 2
    Tap again · speech engine · about 0.25 s

    The speech engine finishes the sentence and hands the words back. It writes exactly what it heard, filler words included.

    Speech engine writes Um, call him at five, actually, at five thirty.
  3. 3
    Tidy-up rules · coordinator · a few milliseconds

    Fixed rules remove filler words (“um”, “uh”, “you know”), fix capitals and spacing, apply your own spellings (“git hub” becomes “GitHub”) and saved snippets, and handle simple corrections like “Send it to Bob, I mean Alice.” In a terminal they also drop the final period.

    After the rules Um, Call him at five, actually, at five thirty.
  4. 4
    AI editor · optional · about 0.3 s, up to 1 s when it rewrites

    A small language model on your computer gets one narrow job: merge the self-corrections the rules missed. It answers either “OK, leave it” or a corrected sentence. If it fails, runs too long, or drops too many of your words, Sunoto keeps the text from step 3.

    After the AI editor Call him at five, actually, at five thirty.
  5. 5
    Paste · coordinator

    Sunoto checks that you are still in the window you were in when you tapped, pastes the text, then puts back whatever was on your clipboard before.

    Lands in your app Call him at five thirty.

What the status pill says

The pill is the only thing Sunoto shows on screen. While recording it stays small: a red dot, a sound meter, and a hint that fades once you start talking. It never shows the speech engine’s guesses while you talk, because they are often wrong where the final text is right. After the second tap it shows a working line, and a check mark when the text is pasted. Words appear only when there is something to report.

First-time setup on a Mac

The Mac is the main focus right now. Installation is one command pasted into Terminal; everything after that is guided.

  1. Run the installerIt puts Sunoto.app in the user’s Applications folder and adds it as a Login Item, so it starts at login.
  2. Speech model downloadsAbout 1.25 GB, once: under half a minute on a fast connection. It is a half-size copy of the original model with the same results.
  3. AI editor stays offIt is optional and off by default. sunoto setup --with-llm downloads its 2.7 GB model and turns it on.
  4. Grant two permissionsAn onboarding panel asks for Accessibility, then Microphone, with an Allow button per row.
  5. Click DoneSunoto restarts once and the installer prints “Sunoto is ready”.
PermissionWhy Sunoto needs itWithout it
AccessibilityTo notice Right Option while you are in another app, paste into the app you are using, and check whether the cursor is in a password field.The shortcut does nothing and text cannot be inserted. The pill says “hotkey blocked”.
MicrophoneTo hear you.Nothing is recorded. The pill says “waiting for microphone access”.

Permission problems were the most common Mac bug in the project’s history: after an update, macOS would quietly stop delivering the shortcut. Setup now signs the app with a stable identity created on that machine, so later updates keep their permissions.

Guardrails built in

Sunoto types into other people’s apps, so most of its design is about never putting text in the wrong place or doing something the user did not intend.

  • Private by constructionAudio and text never leave the computer. There is no server.
  • Never presses Enter or TabA dictated line becomes plain text with spaces, so it can never send a chat message or run a command in a terminal on its own.
  • Right window or nowhereSunoto remembers which window you were in when you tapped to finish. If you switched since, it leaves the text on the clipboard and says so.
  • Stays out of password fieldsOn a Mac, it refuses to insert into a password box and does not copy the text there either.
  • Gives your clipboard backIt pastes through the clipboard, then restores what you had copied before.
  • Microphone is on only while you dictateYour tap turns it on and it goes off as soon as the text is pasted, so the orange dot in the menu bar means Sunoto is listening. Words spoken in the first tenth of a second after the tap can be missed.
  • The AI editor has a short leashIt may only merge self-corrections. If its answer drops too many words, errors, or takes too long, Sunoto uses the rules-only text.
  • Crashes are recoverableIf a helper stops, the coordinator restarts it, waiting a bit longer after each failure.

Known gaps

Things a product decision or more engineering work would change.

  • The AI editor can over-edit Its safety check catches most bad rewrites but not all. Found while writing this page:
    Said: “We should ask the design team, sorry, the product team, to review the onboarding copy.” Inserted: “We should ask the design team, sorry, The product team, to review the onboarding copy.”
  • Dictated text is saved in the local logOn by default to help with debugging. For a release this is a privacy concern; one setting replaces the text with its length and a fingerprint.
  • Built for EnglishThe tidy-up rules and the AI editor’s instructions are English only.
  • No spoken formattingSaying “new line”, “comma” or “bullet point” types those words. Punctuation comes only from the speech engine.
  • Short wait after start-upThe speech engine needs a few seconds to load on a Mac and up to about 30 seconds on Linux. The pill shows this; the shortcut works once it clears.
  • AI editor is off by defaultIt adds about a second per dictation, needs a 2.7 GB download, and occasionally over-edits, so new installs leave it off.
  • Linux is for developers todayInstall is a script that builds from source, and Wayland desktops need the shortcut set up by hand.
  • No settings windowPreferences such as the shortcut, custom spellings and snippets live in a text file and need a restart to apply.

Mac and Linux

The coordinator, the tidy-up rules and the AI editor are shared. The parts that touch the operating system are written separately for each.

Words you will hear from engineers

The same parts, by the names used in code, logs and planning docs.

daemon
The coordinator: the program that is always running in the background. File name sunoto-daemon.
sidecar
A helper program the coordinator starts and exchanges short text messages with.
ASR
Automatic speech recognition: the speech engine.
Parakeet, Nemotron
The two speech models: Parakeet on Mac, Nemotron on Linux.
LLM polish
The AI editor. “LLM” means large language model.
deterministic polish
The tidy-up rules. Deterministic means the same input always gives the same output.
overlay
The status pill.
partials
The speech engine’s running guesses while you are still talking. Kept as a backup for the final text, never shown.
hotkey, event tap
The shortcut and the macOS mechanism that listens for it.
TCC
The macOS privacy system behind the permission prompts.
keepalive, warm-up
Small background pings that keep the AI editor ready, so it does not start cold.
Metal, MLX, CUDA
Ways of running models on the graphics chip: Metal and MLX on Mac, CUDA on NVIDIA.
focus
The window and text field your keystrokes currently go to.
